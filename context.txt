If I want to make money on the 15 minute gold market on kalshi, specifically looking to make money off the volatility with as much upside for as little risk as possible what is a quant strategy that could be implemented which does not require super fast latency (no super high frequency strategies)
For Kalshi's 15-minute Gold market (KXGOLD15M), I would build a strategy around volatility-adjusted directional probability, market mispricing, and asymmetric risk management.

I would specifically avoid traditional high-frequency trading, simple momentum chasing, and trying to predict every tiny gold movement.

The goal would be:

Capture opportunities when gold moves enough to create a meaningful probability imbalance.

Identify when Kalshi's YES/NO prices haven't adjusted appropriately to that movement.

Enter positions with a favorable expected value.

Limit downside to a predetermined amount per trade.

Make relatively few, high-conviction trades instead of trading every 15-minute window.

There is an important distinction: you cannot simultaneously maximize upside and minimize risk without sacrificing something. But you can build a strategy with limited per-trade downside and potentially attractive risk-adjusted returns.

1. Understand the market we're trading
The market I am referring to is KXGOLD15M.

Its settlement mechanism is particularly important:

A new market opens every 15 minutes.

It establishes a starting gold price.

At expiration, it compares the ending gold price against the starting price.

YES wins if the ending price is greater than or equal to the starting price.

NO wins if the ending price is lower.

The settlement uses Pyth's gold price feed and its one-minute candle closes, rather than simply using whatever spot-gold price you see on TradingView.

8. An additional strategy I would investigate: Volatility shock reversal
There is another approach that I think is worth testing independently.

Rather than following volatility expansion, attempt to exploit overreaction.

The hypothesis:

Gold experiences an unusually large movement.

Kalshi's odds rapidly move toward one side.

The underlying movement begins losing momentum.

The market has overestimated the probability that the movement will persist until settlement.

Buy the opposite side if its price is sufficiently attractive.

For example:

Gold spikes upward.

YES increases from 48¢ to 82¢.

Gold stops advancing and starts consolidating.

Your model estimates YES at 68%, rather than the market's 82%.

NO is available at 20¢.

Your model estimates NO at 32%.

You might buy NO if the expected edge survives fees and execution costs.

This is not simply betting against momentum. It requires evidence that the market's implied probability has overshot the actual probability.

I would keep this as a separate strategy initially, because combining momentum and reversal signals prematurely can make it difficult to identify where the profitability actually comes from.

I think this is exactly the right question to ask before writing any code. I would actually challenge several parts of my previous recommendation before investing time in implementation.

There is a big difference between a mathematically sound trading model and a profitable trading strategy. A volatility model can estimate probabilities correctly and still lose money on Kalshi because of transaction fees, spreads, adverse selection, and the fact that other traders may already be exploiting the same information.

I don't want to give you a sophisticated-looking bot that generates hundreds of trades, reports a positive theoretical expected value, and slowly loses your bankroll.

My standard for proceeding would be:

There must be a defensible reason the inefficiency exists.

There must be evidence that the underlying predictive signal contains information.

The signal must survive Kalshi's actual execution costs.

It must demonstrate positive returns on data that wasn't used to develop it.

It must remain profitable under pessimistic assumptions about fills and slippage.

And I want to distinguish three things throughout this analysis:

Established: Supported by financial mathematics, empirical research, or documented market mechanics.

Plausible: Has a defensible economic or statistical rationale, but needs testing on KXGOLD15M.

Unproven: An idea that should not be trusted with money until it passes an experiment.

There is no honest way to promise that a strategy won't lose. The objective is to establish enough evidence before risking capital that we're not simply gambling with a statistical-looking interface.

2. What does academic research tell us about exploiting Kalshi?
There is another particularly relevant study:

Makers and Takers: The Economics of the Kalshi Prediction Market

Authors:

Constantin Bürgi

Wanying Deng

Karl Whelan

Published through the Centre for Economic Policy Research (CEPR), with a revised version dated January 2026.

The researchers analyzed transaction-level data covering more than 300,000 Kalshi contracts. 

CEPR

Their findings are important:

Kalshi prices contain meaningful information.

Prices become more accurate as markets approach expiration.

Low-priced contracts exhibit a favorite-longshot bias: they win less frequently than necessary to break even after fees.

Makers generally earn higher returns than takers.

There is evidence of behavioral pricing distortions.

This is not a study specifically of KXGOLD15M, so we cannot assume its findings translate directly to gold.

Nevertheless, it gives us a much better foundation for thinking about where an edge might exist.

What this means for our strategy
I would make two changes to my original proposal.

First: Don't blindly buy cheap contracts.

Imagine a contract costs 10 cents.

It might look attractive because you can potentially make 90 cents on a 10-cent investment.

But if its actual probability of winning is 7%, you're losing money in expectation.

The fact that a contract is cheap does not mean it's undervalued.

Second: Investigate maker execution.

This is especially interesting.

If other traders are consistently paying to take liquidity, there may be an opportunity to improve our entry price by posting resting limit orders.

However, there is a catch: a limit order can get filled precisely when the market is moving against us. This is called adverse selection.

We need to measure the profitability of actual maker fills, not just assume that avoiding taker fees creates an edge.

9. What I would consider proof that the strategy works
I would establish several gates before allowing it to trade real money.

Predictive validation

The model must demonstrate better probability calibration and out-of-sample predictive performance than the market-implied baseline.

I would use Brier score and log loss, alongside calibration plots.

Economic validation

Simulated trades must be profitable after:

Taker or maker fees.

Bid/ask spreads.

Slippage.

Unfilled limit orders.

Adverse selection.

A profitable prediction model is not enough.

Statistical validation

The lower bound of a 95% confidence interval for net expectancy should be positive.

We should also check performance across different months and volatility regimes rather than relying on one favorable period.

Live paper validation

Run the strategy against live order books for at least several weeks.

Compare its simulated fills against actual subsequent market behavior.

Small-capital deployment

Only then begin trading with very small positions.

Keep a hard maximum loss per day and never use Kelly sizing until the probability model is sufficiently validated.

I would also retract two recommendations from my earlier answer:

The 0.8, 1.3 and 2.0 volatility regime thresholds were illustrative, not empirically established.

The five-cent minimum expected edge was a proposed starting threshold, not a proven optimal trading threshold.

Both should be determined using historical out-of-sample experiments.

Likewise, I would not implement fractional Kelly at this stage. Incorrect probability estimates can make Kelly sizing extremely dangerous.
